**Can you lick a perfectly spherical lollipop into the shape of the Earth?**

Ignore mountains and oceans. At planetary scale, Earth is approximately an
*oblate spheroid*: slightly wider around the equator than from pole to pole.
Imagine shrinking that same proportion onto a round sweet. The task sounds like
a joke, but it immediately asks three serious questions:

1. How much candy must be removed?
2. Where on the surface should it be removed?
3. If a lick leaves an elongated mark, which way should that mark point?

The first is a volume calculation. The second is a problem of assembling a
global shape from many local patches. The third is the trapdoor. Once the
contact patch has a direction, its state is not just a dot on the sphere. We
must also remember an arrow lying flat against the surface.

Carry that arrow around a closed route without deliberately twisting it. When
it returns to its starting point, it may point in a different direction. This
is not slippage; it is curvature. The effect is called *holonomy*, but the name
matters less than the picture: on a curved surface, “keep pointing straight”
depends on the path you take.

This note follows that chain all the way to a formula for the change in the
removal footprint. It explains the [technical paper](/papers/anisotropic-kernel-control-s2.pdf);
the [source and experiments](https://github.com/hhungxun/physics-writeups/tree/main/sources/anisotropic-kernel-control-s2)
let you reproduce the numbers. The model describes small, additive radial
removal on a fixed sphere. Actual candy dissolution would need its own
experimental contact law.

## Knowing how much is not knowing where

The flattening of an ellipsoid is the fractional difference between its
equatorial and polar radii:

$$
f=\frac{a_E-b_E}{a_E}.
$$

WGS84, the reference model used by GPS, specifies an inverse flattening of
298.257223563. In other words, the difference is small—about one part in 298—but
not zero. Keep the lollipop's equatorial radius equal to its original radius
and shorten the polar radius by this fraction. The sphere and target then
differ in volume by

$$
\Delta V=\frac{4\pi}{3}R_0^3f.
$$

That tells us the total amount of candy. It cannot tell us a licking strategy,
for the same reason that knowing how much paint is in a tin does not tell us
what picture to paint.
Removing that amount uniformly simply gives a smaller sphere. Removing more
near the equator makes the shape change in the wrong direction.

We need a *field*: simply a rule assigning an amount of removal to every point.

Let the colatitude be the angle measured down from the north pole. The target
spheroid has radial coordinate

$$
r_f(\theta)=R_0
\left(\sin^2\theta+\frac{\cos^2\theta}{(1-f)^2}\right)^{-1/2}.
$$

Expand in the small flattening. To first order, the desired removal is

$$
\delta_\star(\theta)=R_0f\cos^2\theta.
$$

At a pole the cosine squared is one. At the equator it is zero. The geometry
now tells us where the mass calculation was silent.

This distinction matters later: the cosine-squared field is the **first-order
target**, rather than the exact radial difference for the ellipsoid. At second
order, a term proportional to cosine to the fourth power appears.

## The target is simpler than it looks

Spherical harmonics are the natural “notes” of a sphere. Just as a musical tone
can be decomposed into a fundamental and overtones, a pattern painted on a
sphere can be decomposed into broad and fine angular patterns.

Our target has an especially short expansion. The second Legendre polynomial
satisfies

$$
P_2(t)=\frac{3t^2-1}{2},
\qquad
\cos^2\theta=\frac13+\frac23P_2(\cos\theta).
$$

The constant belongs to harmonic degree zero. The second term belongs to
degree two. There are no other degrees in the linearized target.

The target therefore asks for only two notes: a uniform part and one broad
pole-versus-equator pattern. Hitting those two notes is still hard. A localized lick
is a narrow patch and generally contains many harmonic degrees. Several patches
must add up to approximate a very simple global shape. Good approximation means
both building the desired low-degree pattern and controlling the unwanted
high-degree content.

The coefficients also have a physical restriction: a lick removes candy and
cannot put it back to cancel an earlier mistake, which is why they have to be
non-negative.

## A dot cannot remember which way the lick points

Begin with a circular contact patch. To place it on the sphere, we need its
centre and its size. Rotating it around its centre changes nothing.

Now stretch it into an ellipse. Two patches can have the same centre and the
same widths, while one points north–south and the other east–west. They remove
different fields. The contact point has stopped being a complete state.

At a point on the sphere, imagine laying down two tiny perpendicular arrows:
one along the long axis of the lick and one along its short axis. Both arrows
touch the surface rather than pointing through it; mathematicians call them
*tangent vectors*. Together with the outward-pointing normal, they form a
complete three-dimensional orientation, written as a rotation matrix:

$$
R=[e_1\ e_2\ x]\in \operatorname{SO}(3).
$$

The first two columns specify the contact axes; the last specifies its centre.
The phrase *oriented orthonormal frame bundle* means nothing more mysterious
than “every surface point, together with every possible pair of perpendicular
unit arrows attached there.”

The identification with the rotation group is concrete. Every rotation matrix
has three orthonormal columns, and its first two columns lie in the tangent
plane at its third column. Conversely, a positively oriented tangent basis and
its normal produce exactly such a matrix.

If we erase the two tangent arrows, many different orientations collapse to the
same point. This compact statement records that forgetting operation:

$$
S^2\cong\operatorname{SO}(3)/\operatorname{SO}(2).
$$

There is one subtlety. An ellipse does not distinguish an axis from its
reverse. Turning it through 180 degrees gives the same patch. A full frame
therefore contains a little more information than the footprint needs. Keeping
that redundant frame is convenient because transport acts on frames directly.

## Give the lick a bell-shaped footprint

Real contact marks do not end at a perfectly sharp boundary: they are strongest
near the centre and fade outward. We model that fading with a Gaussian, the
familiar bell curve, stretched into an ellipse.

To write a flat bell curve on a round surface, first unroll a small
neighbourhood onto the tangent plane. The *logarithm map* is the precise version
of this local unrolling. It sends a nearby point to a two-component arrow whose
length is the distance travelled along the sphere.

In polar tangent coordinates, the kernel is

$$
K(r,\phi)=\frac{\chi(r)}{Z_{a,b}}
\exp\left[-\frac{r^2}{2}\big(a+b\cos 2\phi\big)\right],
\qquad a>|b|.
$$

You can read the parameters without doing the integral. The number $a$ sets
the average narrowness. The number $b$ says how unequal the two widths are.
When $b=0$, the two widths match and orientation becomes irrelevant. As
$|b|$ grows, the mark becomes more elongated and more sensitive to rotation.

The cutoff keeps the kernel inside a cap smaller than the antipodal distance.
This avoids ambiguity in the logarithm map at the opposite pole. We use a
smooth cutoff rather than an abrupt edge:

$$
\chi(r)=\exp\left[-\frac{r^2}{r_c^2-r^2}\right]
\quad (r<r_c),
$$

with zero value outside. Its rapid decay makes the boundary smooth.

The normalization makes the integral over the sphere equal to one. It must use
the spherical area element,

$$
dA=\sin r\,dr\,d\phi,
$$

rather than the planar element. Otherwise the removal strength would depend on
an incorrect mass normalization.

The Gaussian is specified relative to the chosen frame. Rotating the frame
while holding its principal-axis matrix fixed rotates the physical footprint.
Rotating the coordinates and transforming the matrix at the same time would
merely describe the same footprint in a different basis.

## Walk an arrow around the sphere

Imagine sliding an arrow along the lollipop. Keep it flat against the surface,
and never deliberately rotate it left or right. This rule is called *parallel
transport*. It is the curved-surface version of keeping an arrow parallel to
itself while moving it across a sheet of paper.

For a unit sphere embedded in ordinary three-dimensional space, the equation
is particularly simple:

$$
\dot v=-(v\cdot\dot x)x.
$$

The derivative points along the normal. Its tangential component is zero,
which expresses the absence of an imposed in-plane twist. Differentiating
the dot product of the arrow and the normal shows that tangency is preserved.
The arrow's length is preserved too.

Now take it around a closed latitude. The arrow returns to the same point,
but generally at a different angle. Gauss–Bonnet relates this holonomy to
the enclosed area. On a unit sphere,

$$
\psi=2\pi(1-\cos\theta_0)\pmod{2\pi}.
$$

At a colatitude of 45 degrees the answer is approximately 1.840302369021
radians, or 105.44 degrees.

The phrase “without twisting” is local. It governs each tiny step, not the
whole trip. Curvature prevents all those locally straight choices from fitting
together globally.

## Turn the returning arrow into a measurable difference

Holonomy tells us an angle, but an angle is not yet a physical consequence. A
perfectly circular lick can rotate by any amount and look identical. An
elliptical lick changes. We need a number that measures how different the two
removal patterns are.

Compare the original footprint with the rotated one at the same centre. Square
their point-by-point difference and add it over the whole sphere:

$$
D_\psi^2=\int_{S^2}(K-K_\psi)^2\,dA.
$$

This is the $L^2$ mismatch: zero means identical removal everywhere, while a
larger value means a larger total disagreement. It is easier to calculate the
overlap first:

$$
C(\psi)=\int_{S^2}K K_\psi\,dA.
$$

Rotation preserves the norm, so the mismatch is twice the difference between
self-overlap and rotated overlap.

The angular dependence looks awkward until we multiply the two bell curves.
Their exponent contains two cosine terms, which combine through

$$
\cos(2\phi)+\cos(2\phi-2\psi)
=2\cos\psi\cos(2\phi-\psi).
$$

The remaining angular average is a standard special function, the modified
Bessel function $I_0$. Naming it lets a two-dimensional surface calculation
collapse to one ordinary integral over distance from the contact centre:

$$
C(\psi)=\frac{2\pi}{Z_{a,b}^2}
\int_0^{r_c}
\chi(r)^2e^{-ar^2}I_0(br^2\cos\psi)\sin r\,dr.
$$

Consequently,

$$
D_\psi^2=\frac{4\pi}{Z_{a,b}^2}
\int_0^{r_c}\chi(r)^2e^{-ar^2}
\left[I_0(br^2)-I_0(br^2\cos\psi)\right]\sin r\,dr.
$$

This is the paper's central formula. A computer can evaluate it quickly, but
its structure already tells us the important qualitative facts.
The Bessel function is even and increases with the magnitude of its argument.
The mismatch therefore vanishes exactly at multiples of a half-turn, and
is largest at a quarter-turn modulo a half-turn.

Here is the exception that a purely qualitative description can miss.
At a colatitude of 60 degrees, the enclosed cap has area pi. The frame returns
rotated by 180 degrees. Yet the removal footprint is exactly the same.
Nonzero frame holonomy does not always mean nonzero actuation response.

## Almost circular means almost insensitive—but at what rate?

If a footprint is only slightly elongated, intuition says orientation should
matter only slightly. That is true, but “slightly” could mean proportional to
the elongation, its square, or something subtler. The overlap formula decides.

The Bessel series begins with a constant and a quadratic term. After
subtracting the two Bessel functions, the constant cancels. With a fixed
mean precision and a fixed rotation angle,

$$
D_\psi^2=
\frac{\pi b^2\sin^2\psi}{Z_{a,0}^2}
\int_0^{r_c}\chi(r)^2e^{-ar^2}r^4\sin r\,dr
+O(b^4).
$$

Thus the mismatch itself grows linearly with small elongation, while its square
grows quadratically. The rotation contributes a sine factor: no response at
zero and 180 degrees, and the strongest response at 90 degrees. The coefficient
comes from the stated radial integral rather than a curve fit.

In the numerical experiment, the mean precision is 16 and the cutoff radius
is 1.2 radians. We use the 45-degree latitude loop and write the contrast as
epsilon equal to b divided by a. The expansion predicts a mismatch of
1.040885 times the absolute contrast at leading order.

| Precision contrast | Relative mismatch |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 |
| 0.01 | 0.008618 |
| 0.02 | 0.017236 |
| 0.10 | 0.086272 |
| 0.40 | 0.350946 |
| 0.60 | 0.539096 |

Doubling the smallest contrast almost exactly doubles the mismatch. The
measured logarithmic slope is 0.999973, extremely close to the predicted value
of one. If we plotted the *squared* mismatch, the slope would be two; keeping
those two quantities distinct is a small but important bookkeeping check.

## Can a stronger wrong-way lick compensate?

Suppose the desired mark points north–south, but the available contact points
diagonally. Could we simply press harder—or, in the lollipop story, lick more?

The best non-negative strength is the overlap divided by the self-overlap:

$$
\alpha_\star=\frac{C(\psi)}{C(0)}.
$$

Even after choosing that strength, the squared error is

$$
C(0)-\frac{C(\psi)^2}{C(0)}.
$$

Cauchy–Schwarz, a standard inequality comparing overlap with size, makes this
error positive unless the two footprints are proportional.
Because both integrate to one, proportionality would mean equality.
A wrong orientation outside the half-turn symmetry therefore has an
irreducible one-contact error. Admitting the right orientation reduces that
error to zero.

This is a deliberately controlled comparison: one target kernel, one available
kernel, the same widths, and a freely adjustable non-negative strength.
It gives a precise example of why orientation matters without claiming that
every anisotropic strategy beats every isotropic strategy.

## From one lick to a strategy

One contact cannot build a planet. For a finite menu of possible locations and
orientations, choose a non-negative strength for each footprint. The goal is to
minimise shape error without using an unreasonable amount of total removal:

$$
J(\alpha)=
\left\|d-\sum_j\alpha_jK_j\right\|_2^2+
\lambda\sum_j\alpha_j,
\qquad \alpha_j\geq0.
$$

The paper proves that this finite optimisation has a solution. The key reason
is physical as well as mathematical: all strengths are non-negative and every
kernel has total mass one, so a bounded-error strategy cannot hide an
unbounded amount of removal through cancellation.

The resulting removal pattern is unique. The individual recipe need not be:
two different combinations of overlapping contacts can paint the same final
field. This is like mixing the same colour from different proportions of paint.

The numerical benchmark fits the dimensionless target equal to the square of
the vertical coordinate. We fix twelve centres on the sphere. One dictionary
offers a single orientation at each centre; the other offers four.

| Candidate atoms | Active strengths | Relative error on fitting grid | Relative error on finer grid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | 12 | 0.471593 | 0.471593 |
| 48 | 20 | 0.436528 | 0.436528 |

The 48-choice menu contains every option from the 12-choice menu, so its best
error cannot become worse. The computation finds that it becomes modestly
better. Repeating the evaluation on a finer grid checks that the improvement
is not just a pixel-resolution illusion.

These are fairly large residuals. Twelve fixed centres and a prescribed
footprint do not approximate the global target particularly well.
The experiment checks a finite fitting problem; it does not solve the
original lollipop design problem.

## What this calculation establishes—and what it does not

The result has three independent checks rather than one impressive-looking
output number.

First, we integrated parallel transport and halved the step size repeatedly.
The errors decrease at approximately fourth order. A 20,000-step run gives
the latitude holonomy to about twelve decimal places.

Second, we computed the mismatch two ways: through the one-dimensional
Bessel formula and by integrating the squared difference of the actual
kernels over radial and angular coordinates. The largest discrepancy in
the table is below 1.3 times ten to the minus fourteen. Refining the direct
quadrature produces the same displayed values.

Third, we checked the non-negative fitting conditions and evaluated the
fitted strengths on a finer sphere grid. These checks support the stated
finite computations.

The unresolved control problem is more ambitious: choose both the contacts
**and the route that carries their orientations**. If all footprints were
fixed in advance, their order would not matter. A route matters here because
transport changes the attached arrows and therefore changes later footprints.
An actuator that deliberately resets its orientation at every contact would
remove this history dependence.

An actual tool might twist, slip, remove material during travel, or alter
the geometry enough that the reference sphere is no longer adequate.
Those extensions require additional assumptions and calculations.
The result here is narrower: for the specified fixed-sphere, additive-removal
model, we can calculate exactly how a frame rotation changes one footprint and
solve a finite non-negative fitting problem. It does not claim to solve the
evolving physical lollipop.

## What is new here, and what came before

Directional wavelets already describe orientation-sensitive functions on the
sphere. [Antoine and collaborators](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1063-5203(02)00507-9)
provide an early implementation and analysis.

[Cohen and collaborators](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/cohen19d.html)
make the role of local frames explicit in gauge-equivariant convolution,
including the path dependence of transported filters.
[Schonsheck, Dong, and Lai](https://doi.org/10.1137/21M1407616) construct
parallel-transport convolution on manifolds.

In a different setting,
[Tam and Cheng](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmatprotec.2010.01.012) show that
polishing paths and elliptic contacts affect material removal.

The geometry itself—directional functions on spheres, tangent frames, parallel
transport, and holonomy—is established mathematics. The contribution of this
note is the particular bridge between those ideas and a non-negative removal
model: an explicit one-integral orientation response for the chosen kernel, its
half-turn exception, its weak-anisotropy law, and a finite fitting benchmark.

So, can we lick the spherical lollipop into Earth? This paper does not yet give
a practical route. It does something earlier and cleaner: it shows why a route
cannot be described by contact points alone. Once a lick has a long axis, the
sphere remembers how that axis travelled. Curvature becomes part of the
actuator.
