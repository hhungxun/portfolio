A random walker forgets. At every step it throws away the coin result and keeps
only a new position distribution. A quantum walker cannot update probabilities
that way because quantum evolution must remain unitary. It carries a coin state,
keeps complex amplitudes for every path, and postpones measurement. Paths that
arrive at the same position can then reinforce or cancel one another.

That one change replaces diffusive spreading,
$\sigma\propto\sqrt t$, with ballistic spreading, $\sigma\propto t$. But “a
quantum walk is faster” is an incomplete lesson. The speed comes from a
specific spectral and interference structure. It does not mean that every
quantum-walk algorithm beats every classical algorithm, nor that measuring a
far-away position solves a computational problem by itself.

## Begin with the classical recurrence

For an unbiased walk on the integers, let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be independent
steps with values $\pm1$. After $t$ steps,

$$
S_t=\sum_{k=1}^tX_k,
\qquad \mathbb E[S_t]=0,
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_t)=t.
$$

The exact probability of reaching $x$ is binomial:

$$
P_t(x)=2^{-t}\binom{t}{(t+x)/2},
$$

when $t+x$ is even, and zero otherwise. Parity matters: after an even number of
steps, only even positions are reachable.

At large $t$, the central limit theorem gives a Gaussian envelope with width
$\sqrt t$. The reason is already in the variance calculation. Independent
increments add variances, not standard deviations.

The same scaling appears from a continuum limit. If the lattice spacing is
$\ell$, the time step is $\tau$, and $\ell^2/(2\tau)\to D$, then the master
equation approaches

$$
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}=D\frac{\partial^2P}{\partial x^2}.
$$

A point source evolves into the heat kernel

$$
P(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right).
$$

Diffusion smooths and irreversibly loses information. Running the heat equation
backward magnifies short-wavelength noise. That is precisely what a closed
quantum system is forbidden to do: its time evolution must be reversible.

## Quantizing the walk requires an extra space

The obvious rule “send half the amplitude left and half right” is not unitary:
it cannot remember where the amplitude came from. A discrete-time quantum walk
adds a two-dimensional coin space

$$
\mathcal H=\mathcal H_{\mathrm{position}}\otimes
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{coin}}.
$$

Using coin basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, one step consists of a
coin rotation followed by a conditional translation. For the Hadamard coin,

$$
H=\frac1{\sqrt2}
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

and

$$
S|x,0\rangle=|x-1,0\rangle,
\qquad
S|x,1\rangle=|x+1,1\rangle.
$$

The complete step operator is

$$
U=S(I\otimes H).
$$

Both $H$ and $S$ are unitary, so $U$ is unitary. No path information is
discarded until measurement.

The first step resembles a classical split. The distinction appears once
different histories meet. At a later site, one amplitude may arrive through
the coin state $|0\rangle$ and another through $|1\rangle$; the next Hadamard
operation mixes them. Their complex signs and phases determine whether the
outgoing amplitude grows or vanishes.

## The initial phase is physical

Consider the balanced initial coin

$$
|c_\phi\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Every value of $\phi$ gives equal initial probabilities for the two coin
states. Yet the later position distribution can be strongly asymmetric because
the relative phase changes interference. For the Hadamard walk, $\phi=\pi/2$
produces a symmetric distribution.

## Ballistic spreading comes from phases that remain coherent

For a translation-invariant walk, Fourier transform position into quasi-momentum
$k$. Translation becomes multiplication by $e^{\pm ik}$, so the infinite walk
decomposes into independent $2\times2$ unitary matrices $U(k)$. Their
eigenvalues have the form $e^{\pm i\omega(k)}$.

A localized state contains many $k$ values. After $t$ steps each mode acquires
phase $e^{\pm it\omega(k)}$. Stationary-phase analysis says the largest
long-time contributions occur along rays

$$
\frac{x}{t}=\pm\frac{d\omega}{dk},
$$

the group velocities of the two bands. Because these velocities are finite and
nonzero, the dominant fronts move a distance proportional to $t$. This is the
spectral reason for ballistic spreading.

For the symmetric Hadamard walk, the rescaled position $X_t/t$ approaches a
non-Gaussian limit supported inside
$[-1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2]$. In particular,

$$
\frac{\sigma_q(t)}{t}\longrightarrow
\sqrt{1-\frac1{\sqrt2}}\approx0.541,
$$

whereas $\sigma_c(t)/\sqrt t\to1$ for the classical unbiased walk. The two
limits scale different random variables: $X_t/t$ in the coherent case and
$X_t/\sqrt t$ in the diffusive case. This distinction is made precise by
[Konno's one-dimensional limit theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0206103).

The quantum speed is therefore not a larger diffusion constant. A diffusion
constant still gives width proportional to $\sqrt t$. The quantum walk belongs
to a different scaling regime.

### What happens if we measure every step?

Suppose the coin is measured after each application of $U$ and the result is
forgotten. The off-diagonal coherences in the coin-position density matrix are
removed. Alternative paths no longer meet as amplitudes; they meet as ordinary
probabilities. For the Hadamard coin this repeated dephasing recovers a
classical-like diffusive walk.

This gives an operational meaning to coherence. It is not an extra force
pushing the walker outward. It is the ability to preserve and later compare
the phases of paths that would otherwise be distinguishable.

## Continuous time removes the coin, not interference

On a graph $G$, a continuous-time classical walk evolves through a Markov
generator, often the graph Laplacian $L$:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=-\gamma L\mathbf p.
$$

The exponential $e^{-\gamma Lt}$ damps modes with positive eigenvalues and
drives probability toward equilibrium when the graph permits it.

A continuous-time quantum walk instead uses a Hermitian graph operator as a
Hamiltonian:

$$
i\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle=H|\psi(t)\rangle,
\qquad
|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iHt}|\psi(0)\rangle.
$$

$H$ may be an adjacency matrix or a Laplacian, depending on convention and the
physical model. The imaginary unit does not simply “make a classical matrix
unitary.” Rather, Hermiticity of $H$ makes its exponential unitary. Each graph
eigenmode acquires a phase instead of being exponentially damped.

On the infinite line, choosing a nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian yields amplitudes
proportional to Bessel functions:

$$
\langle x|\psi(t)\rangle=i^xJ_x(2\gamma t),
\qquad
P_t(x)=J_x^2(2\gamma t),
$$

up to an overall phase and convention for $H$. The Bessel profile again has a
ballistic front. [Konno's continuous-time analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0408140)
places this contrast with the classical central-limit scaling on a precise
limit-theorem footing.

## A wide distribution is not yet an algorithm

Quantum walks become algorithmic when graph structure makes a desired answer
interfere constructively while unwanted answers cancel. The performance claim
must then include the cost of preparing the state, implementing the walk,
querying an oracle, and extracting the result.

Two examples show the range of the idea:

- The Shenvi–Kempe–Whaley walk searches a hypercube with
  $O(\sqrt N)$ oracle calls, a Grover-like query advantage
  [in that oracle model](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0210064).
- Childs showed that scattering in a continuous-time walk on a sparse graph can
  encode universal quantum computation
  [in the graph itself](https://arxiv.org/abs/0806.1972).

Neither result follows merely from $\sigma_q\propto t$. Ballistic transport is
a useful primitive; computation requires a problem-dependent interference
design and a complexity analysis.

The same caution applies to mixing. A classical ergodic walk can converge to a
stationary distribution because its nonconstant spectral modes decay. A closed
unitary walk keeps rotating phases and generally does not converge pointwise.
Researchers therefore study time-averaged distributions, hitting probabilities,
state transfer, or decohered variants, each with a definition chosen for the
task.

## What the course report establishes

The report develops the classical lattice walk and its heat-equation limit,
introduces the Hilbert-space machinery needed for coherent evolution, constructs
both coined and continuous-time walks, and surveys search, mixing, and state
transfer. Its strongest reusable lesson is the comparison of generators:

$$
\text{classical: }e^{-tL}
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\text{quantum: }e^{-itH}.
$$

One semigroup damps; the other rotates. From that difference follow diffusion
versus interference, stationary behaviour versus persistent oscillation, and
many of the conceptual difficulties in defining quantum hitting and mixing.

The report is a course survey, not a new theorem. Some application sections
summarize proposals whose practical advantage depends on their computational
model and hardware assumptions. The interactive above reproduces the cleanest
claim directly: unitary Hadamard dynamics on the line, with a controlled initial
phase, spreads ballistically while the matched classical walk spreads
diffusively.
