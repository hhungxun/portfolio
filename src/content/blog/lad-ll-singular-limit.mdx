Suppose a complicated theory and its simpler approximation disagree. What has
failed? The approximation might have been truncated too early. The underlying
classical theory might have left its domain of validity. Or the numerical
method might simply be wrong. Calling all three possibilities “breakdown” hides
the question we actually need to answer.

Radiation reaction gives a sharp example. The Abraham–Lorentz (AL) equation
contains an unphysical runaway solution. The Landau–Lifshitz (LL) equation
removes that direction by reduction of order. Meanwhile, sufficiently strong
fields make photon recoil and stochastic emission important, so *any*
deterministic classical equation becomes questionable. Those are different
limits, governed by different dimensionless parameters.

This article builds the smallest model in which we can measure the first limit
exactly and keep it separate from the second.

## Start with the singular limit

In one nonrelativistic spatial dimension, write the external force per unit
mass as $f(t)$. The AL equation is

$$
a(t)=f(t)+\tau_e\dot a(t),
$$

where $\tau_e\simeq6.26\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{s}$ for an electron. It looks
like a small correction because $\tau_e$ is tiny. But rearranging gives

$$
\tau_e\dot a-a=-f.
$$

The small number multiplies the highest derivative. Setting $\tau_e=0$ lowers
the order of the differential equation and removes the initial acceleration as
independent data. That is a **singular perturbation**, not an ordinary small
change to a stable initial-value problem.

With $f=0$, the homogeneous equation has

$$
a_h(t)=C e^{t/\tau_e}.
$$

Unless $C$ is exactly zero, a microscopic error grows exponentially. The
physical prescription is therefore not to choose an arbitrary initial
acceleration. We demand that the acceleration vanish after the force has
disappeared. This selects the non-runaway branch.

## Deriving the exact physical branch

Multiply the equation by the integrating factor $e^{-t/\tau_e}$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-t/\tau_e}a(t)\right)
=-\frac{1}{\tau_e}e^{-t/\tau_e}f(t).
$$

Integrate from the present time $t$ to a late time $R$, then take
$R\rightarrow\infty$ on the non-runaway branch. The boundary term at $R$
vanishes and we obtain

$$
a_{\mathrm{AL}}(t)=\frac{1}{\tau_e}\int_t^\infty
e^{-(s-t)/\tau_e}f(s)\,ds
=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}f(t+\tau_e u)\,du.
$$

The exponential kernel is positive, has unit mass, and has width $\tau_e$.
It averages the force over a very short interval—but that interval points into
the future. The selected branch is therefore pre-accelerating and formally
acausal. Its tiny timescale does not turn it into a causal law; it only makes
the acausal interval extraordinarily difficult to resolve experimentally.

Expand the shifted force:

$$
f(t+\tau_e u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{(\tau_e u)^n}{n!}f^{(n)}(t).
$$

Since $\int_0^\infty e^{-u}u^n\,du=n!$, the branch has the formal derivative
series

$$
a_{\mathrm{AL}}=f+\tau_e\dot f+\tau_e^2\ddot f+\cdots.
$$

Truncating after the first derivative gives the LL approximation in this
model:

$$
a_{\mathrm{LL}}=f+\tau_e\dot f.
$$

This calculation explains both the usefulness and the danger of reduction of
order. It replaces an unstable higher-order evolution with a local expression,
but its accuracy depends on how quickly the force varies.

## A Gaussian makes the comparison countable

Choose

$$
f(t)=f_0e^{-t^2/(2T^2)},\qquad x=\frac{t}{T},\qquad
\epsilon=\frac{\tau_e}{T}.
$$

After dividing acceleration by $f_0$, every profile depends only on $x$ and
$\epsilon$:

$$
\frac{a_{\mathrm{AL}}}{f_0}=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}
e^{-(x+\epsilon u)^2/2}\,du,
\qquad
\frac{a_{\mathrm{LL}}}{f_0}=e^{-x^2/2}(1-\epsilon x).
$$

$\epsilon$ is a timescale ratio. A slow pulse has $\epsilon\ll1$ and a rapidly
varying stress test approaches $\epsilon=1$. Move the slider below. The graph

## An exact error law, not just a fitted slope

Define the normalized RMS discrepancy

$$
D_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=
\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|a_{\mathrm{LL}}-a_{\mathrm{AL}}|^2\,dx}
{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|a_{\mathrm{AL}}|^2\,dx}.
$$

A log–log plot can suggest a power law, but it does not prove one. Fourier
space exposes the structure exactly. With
$\widehat h(\omega)=\int h(x)e^{-i\omega x}\,dx$, the differential equation
and LL truncation give

$$
\widehat a_{\mathrm{AL}}=
\frac{\widehat g}{1-i\epsilon\omega},\qquad
\widehat a_{\mathrm{LL}}=(1+i\epsilon\omega)\widehat g,
$$

where $g(x)=e^{-x^2/2}$. Their difference is

$$
\widehat a_{\mathrm{LL}}-\widehat a_{\mathrm{AL}}
=-\frac{\epsilon^2\omega^2}{1-i\epsilon\omega}\widehat g.
$$

Parseval's identity therefore yields, for every $\epsilon>0$,

$$
D_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=
\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\epsilon^4\omega^4}{1+\epsilon^2\omega^2}e^{-\omega^2}\,d\omega}
{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{1}{1+\epsilon^2\omega^2}e^{-\omega^2}\,d\omega}.
$$

Introduce

$$
J(\epsilon)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{-\omega^2}}{1+\epsilon^2\omega^2}\,d\omega
=\frac{\pi}{\epsilon}e^{1/\epsilon^2}
\operatorname{erfc}(1/\epsilon).
$$

Polynomial division of the numerator then gives the closed expression

$$
\boxed{D_{\mathrm{rms}}^2
=1-\frac{\sqrt\pi(1-\epsilon^2/2)}{J(\epsilon)}}.
$$

The boxed formula suffers cancellation if evaluated naively at very small
$\epsilon$. Expanding it instead gives

$$
D_{\mathrm{rms}}=
\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\epsilon^2
\left[1-\epsilon^2+3\epsilon^4+O(\epsilon^6)\right].
$$

Thus $\sqrt3/2$ is not a fitted decoration. It follows from the exact spectral
identity. In the downloadable calculation, a time-domain Simpson quadrature is
checked against the independent frequency-domain integral at 15 values of
$\epsilon$; the largest RMS discrepancy is $6.48\times10^{-9}$. Doubling the
representative grids changes $D_{\mathrm{rms}}$ by at most
$1.3\times10^{-13}$.

## A second parameter answers a different question

The strong-field quantum parameter is

$$
\chi_e=\frac{e\hbar}{m^3c^4}
\sqrt{-\left(F_{\mu\nu}p^\nu\right)^2}.
$$

Roughly, it measures the field seen in the electron rest frame relative to the
QED critical field. Quantum corrections can become measurable around
$\chi_e\sim0.1$; $\chi_e\sim1$ is a useful guide for strong recoil. Neither
threshold is an abrupt phase transition.

In the normalized prescribed-force model, $\epsilon$ measures the accuracy of
classical reduction of order, while $\chi_e$ flags the adequacy of a
deterministic classical emission model. Adjust them independently below.

For a femtosecond pulse, $\epsilon$ is around $10^{-9}$, so this benchmark's
LL truncation error is around $10^{-18}$. A strong-field experiment can still
have appreciable quantum corrections because its $\chi_e$ need not be small.
[Recent all-optical radiation-reaction measurements](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-67918-8),
for example, report mean quantum parameters around $0.05$–$0.1$: already
interesting for quantum corrections, but not accurately described as
$\chi_e\gtrsim1$.

## What is—and is not—new here

Gaussian-pulse comparisons between AL and LL
[already exist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14809), and the broader fact that
reduction-of-order series may be asymptotic
[is established](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01640). The claim here is narrower:
for this normalized Gaussian benchmark we give an exact
finite-$\epsilon$ RMS error identity, connect it explicitly to the singular
loss of differential order, verify it through independent time- and
frequency-domain computations, and place it on a two-parameter diagnostic map
without conflating $\epsilon$ with $\chi_e$.

The model is one-dimensional, nonrelativistic, and externally prescribed. It
does not prove a relativistic error theorem, compute a photon spectrum, include
beam back-reaction, or resolve the conceptual acausality of the AL physical
branch. Values near $\epsilon=1$ are mathematical stress tests, not attainable
electron pulse durations.

That limited claim is useful precisely because it is auditable. Every equation
needed to reproduce the central result fits on this page; the repository adds
the numerical cross-check and complete manuscript source.
