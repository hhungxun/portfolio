## The question before the equations

Radiation reaction is often discussed as if “Landau–Lifshitz breakdown” named
one phenomenon. It does not. There are two different questions:

1. Does the Landau–Lifshitz (LL) reduction accurately follow the physical,
   non-runaway branch of the Lorentz–Abraham–Dirac (LAD) equation?
2. Even if the classical reduction is accurate, is a deterministic classical
   emission model still appropriate once photon recoil and stochastic emission
   matter?

The paper answers the first question in a deliberately small model, then places
its answer next to the quantum nonlinearity parameter $\chi_e$. The result is
not a new equation of motion. It is a reproducible benchmark for keeping two
meanings of “breakdown” apart.

## Why LAD is difficult to integrate

In covariant form, LAD contains a second proper-time derivative of the
four-velocity. Since velocity is already the derivative of position, the
position equation is third order. Position and velocity at one instant do not
specify a solution; an initial acceleration is also needed.

That extra degree of freedom contains the runaway. With no external force, a
generic solution can contain a term proportional to

$$
\exp(t/\tau_e), \qquad \tau_e \simeq 6.26\times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

for an electron. A numerical integrator that starts off the physical manifold
will therefore amplify tiny errors instead of relaxing them.

The standard physical prescription is a *future* boundary condition: after the
force has disappeared, the acceleration must vanish rather than grow. This
selects the non-runaway branch, but it also makes the selected acceleration
nonlocal in time. The acceleration now depends very slightly on force values
that have not yet arrived. That is pre-acceleration, a consequence of the
boundary condition rather than an instability in the quadrature used below.

## The one-dimensional benchmark

Take the nonrelativistic, one-dimensional Abraham–Lorentz limit, with a force
per unit mass $f(t)=F(t)/m$:

$$
a(t)=f(t)+\tau_e\dot a(t).
$$

The homogeneous solution is the runaway $a_h=C\exp(t/\tau_e)$. To remove it,
impose $a(t)\to0$ after the force has switched off. Solving with that future
condition gives

$$
a_{\mathrm{LAD}}(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}f(t+\tau_e u)\,du.
$$

This formula is the exact physical branch *within this toy limit*. It is also
numerically friendly: there is no unstable initial-value integration to perform.

Now expand the shifted force:

$$
f(t+\tau_e u)=f(t)+\tau_e u\dot f(t)+
\frac{\tau_e^2u^2}{2}\ddot f(t)+\cdots.
$$

Because $\int_0^\infty e^{-u}u^n\,du=n!$, the exact branch has the formal
series

$$
a_{\mathrm{LAD}}=f+\tau_e\dot f+\tau_e^2\ddot f+\cdots.
$$

Keeping only the first two terms is precisely the local LL approximation in
this setting:

$$
a_{\mathrm{LL}}(t)=f(t)+\tau_e\dot f(t).
$$

The important mathematical point is that the LL approximation is a *singular
perturbation reduction*. The small parameter multiplies the highest derivative
in the original equation, so setting $\tau_e=0$ changes the order of the
problem and discards the runaway direction. This is not an ordinary Taylor
perturbation of a stable initial-value problem.

## Choosing a pulse and a dimensionless parameter

The paper uses a smooth Gaussian force, with characteristic duration $T$. The
dimensionless pulse-variation parameter is

$$
\epsilon=\frac{\tau_e}{T}.
$$

Small $\epsilon$ means that the force changes slowly on the radiation-reaction
timescale. Large $\epsilon$ is useful for stressing the approximation,
although $\epsilon\sim1$ is not a realistic electron laboratory regime when
$\tau_e$ is $10^{-24}$ seconds.

For a Gaussian, the error is smooth and its first omitted term is proportional
to $\epsilon^2$. Normalising by the force scale gives the root-mean-square
result

$$
D_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\epsilon^2
\left[1-\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^4)\right],
\qquad D_{\max}\sim\epsilon^2.
$$

The coefficient $\sqrt{3}/2\approx0.866$ is not fitted as an arbitrary
constant. It comes from Gaussian moment integrals and is then checked against
the numerical quadrature. That analytic–numeric agreement is what makes this a
benchmark rather than merely a plot of two curves.

### Where the future integral comes from

It is worth doing the boundary-condition step once, because it is the part most
likely to be hidden by a black-box solver. Rewrite the one-dimensional equation
as

$$
\dot a-\frac{1}{\tau_e}a=-\frac{1}{\tau_e}f(t).
$$

Multiplying by $\exp(-t/\tau_e)$ gives an exact derivative. Integrate from the
present time $t$ to a late time $R$, then send $R\to\infty$ on the branch for
which $a(R)\to0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left[e^{-s/\tau_e}a(s)\right]_{s=t}^{s=R}
  &=-\frac{1}{\tau_e}\int_t^R e^{-s/\tau_e}f(s)\,ds,\\
a(t)&=\frac{1}{\tau_e}\int_t^\infty
 e^{-(s-t)/\tau_e}f(s)\,ds.
\end{aligned}
$$

The substitution $u=(s-t)/\tau_e$ produces the compact formula used above.
This derivation makes two facts visible: the kernel is positive and has unit
mass, and its width is exactly $\tau_e$. The LAD branch is therefore a causal
looking average shifted into the future by the boundary condition; it is not a
second numerical initial condition that we are free to tune.

### A reproducible Gaussian test

For the benchmark, write the pulse as

$$
f(t)=f_0\exp\!\left[-\frac{t^2}{2T^2}\right],
\qquad x=\frac{t}{T},\qquad \epsilon=\frac{\tau_e}{T}.
$$

After dividing accelerations by $f_0$, the exact calculation depends only on
$x$ and $\epsilon$:

$$
\frac{a_{\mathrm{LAD}}}{f_0}=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x+\epsilon u)^2}{2}\right]du,
\qquad
\frac{a_{\mathrm{LL}}}{f_0}=e^{-x^2/2}
\left(1-\epsilon x\right).
$$

This nondimensionalisation is more than cosmetic. It prevents the tiny physical
value of $\tau_e$ from being confused with numerical underflow and lets one
run the same test in seconds, code units, or symbolic algebra. The first omitted
term is $\tau_e^2f''(t)$. For the Gaussian,

$$
f''(t)=\left(\frac{t^2}{T^4}-\frac{1}{T^2}\right)f(t),
$$

so the difference is an $O(\epsilon^2)$ function with the same rapidly decaying
envelope as the pulse. Squaring that expression and integrating Gaussian
moments gives the reported $\sqrt{3}/2$ RMS coefficient; the computer is then
checking a coefficient obtained before the numerical fit.

## What the computation actually checks

The numerical section evaluates the future-weighted LAD integral using composite
Simpson quadrature for $\epsilon\in[0.01,1]$. It compares that value with the
local LL expression and records both an RMS error and a maximum error. Two
reruns change the reported RMS value by less than $4\times10^{-18}$ when the
integration cutoff is varied and less than $2\times10^{-11}$ when the time
grid is refined.

Those checks matter because a numerical slope can be manufactured by an
under-resolved quadrature. Here the leading slope is predicted independently,
the quadrature recovers it, and the first correction is visible over the scan.

## Keeping classical accuracy separate from quantum validity

The second axis is the strong-field quantum parameter $\chi_e$, which roughly
compares the electron’s rest-frame field with the Schwinger field:

$$
\chi_e\simeq \frac{\gamma E_{\mathrm{eff}}}{E_S}.
$$

In this linear benchmark, changing the pulse amplitude or beam energy can move
$\chi_e$ without changing the *normalised* LL–LAD error. The paper therefore
uses an $(\epsilon,\chi_e)$ map as a diagnostic: horizontal motion changes
classical reduction-of-order accuracy, while vertical motion changes whether a
classical deterministic emission model is physically adequate.

For femtosecond pulses, $\epsilon\sim10^{-9}$, so the benchmarked classical
truncation error is around $10^{-18}$. That does not say that a strong-field
experiment is classical. It says that if the experiment disagrees with a
classical prediction, this particular LL truncation error is an implausible
first suspect; quantum emission, beam dynamics, field modelling, or detector
backgrounds deserve attention instead.

## Scope and limits

The result is intentionally narrow. The force is prescribed, the motion is
nonrelativistic and one-dimensional, and the exact physical branch exists in a
closed integral form. The paper does not prove a relativistic error theorem, nor
does it calculate a quantum-emission spectrum. The $\epsilon\sim1$ scan is a
controlled stress test, not a claim that an electron’s $\tau_e$ becomes
macroscopic.

That modest scope is the point: before debating a complicated “breakdown,”
identify which approximation is being tested and build the smallest exact
problem that can measure it.

## Reproduction checklist

To reproduce the central figure, choose a grid in $x=t/T$, evaluate the
future integral on a sufficiently long positive $u$ interval, and compare it
with the closed-form LL expression above. Increase the cutoff until the tail
is negligible; then refine the $x$ grid and verify that the reported RMS slope
does not move. A useful independent check is to plot

$$
\log D_{\mathrm{rms}} \quad\text{against}\quad \log\epsilon.
$$

The small-$\epsilon$ points should approach slope $2$. If the slope is closer
to $1$, the derivative term has probably been omitted; if it is erratic, the
quadrature or time window is under-resolved. These are deliberately boring
checks, but they are what turns a plausible visual comparison into a numerical
claim that another reader can audit.
