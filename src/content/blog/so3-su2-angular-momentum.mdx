The formula

$$
J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle
$$

often arrives in a first quantum-mechanics course as something to memorize.
That presentation reverses the logic. The strange-looking $j(j+1)$ and the
finite list $m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j$ are not guesses fitted to atomic data. They
follow from three structural facts: spatial rotations do not commute,
observable generators are Hermitian, and Hilbert-space norms cannot be
negative.

Hydrogen then adds a second layer. Rotation symmetry determines the angular
functions and their allowed quantum numbers, while the Coulomb potential and
radial boundary conditions quantize the energy. Keeping those layers separate
shows exactly which part of the spectrum comes from symmetry and which part
comes from dynamics.

## Rotations carry an algebra

An infinitesimal rotation by a small vector $\boldsymbol\theta$ acts on a
quantum state through

$$
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=I-\frac{i}{\hbar}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J
+O(|\boldsymbol\theta|^2).
$$

The operators $J_x,J_y,J_z$ are the generators of rotations. Rotating a little
about $x$ and then $y$ does not give precisely the same result as reversing the
order. The mismatch is itself a small rotation about $z$. Algebraically,

$$
[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k.
$$

This is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, equivalently
$\mathfrak{su}(2)$ at the infinitesimal level. It says more than “components
cannot be measured together.” It says that each component generates a motion
on state space, and the commutator records the curvature of composing those
motions.

The natural rotational scalar is

$$
J^2=J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2.
$$

Using $[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B$, one finds $[J^2,J_i]=0$. Thus $J^2$ is the
Casimir operator: within an irreducible representation it has one constant
value, while a chosen component such as $J_z$ distinguishes states inside the
multiplet. We may therefore seek simultaneous eigenstates

$$
J^2|\lambda,m\rangle=\lambda|\lambda,m\rangle,
\qquad J_z|\lambda,m\rangle=\hbar m|\lambda,m\rangle.
$$

## Ladder operators are eigen-operators

We want an operator $A$ that changes the $J_z$ eigenvalue by a fixed amount:

$$
[J_z,A]=\lambda A.
$$

Trying $A=\alpha J_x+\beta J_y$ and inserting the rotation commutators gives
$\lambda=\pm\hbar$ and $\beta=\pm i\alpha$. Up to normalization, the only
solutions are

$$
J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y,
\qquad [J_z,J_\pm]=\pm\hbar J_\pm.
$$

So $J_\pm$ are not magic combinations introduced after the answer is known.
They are eigenvectors of the adjoint action $A\mapsto[J_z,A]$. Applied to a
state, they shift $m$ by one without changing the value of $J^2$.

Two identities make the spectrum visible:

$$
J_-J_+=J^2-J_z^2-\hbar J_z,
\qquad
J_+J_-=J^2-J_z^2+\hbar J_z.
$$

Take the norm of a raised state:

$$
\|J_+|\lambda,m\rangle\|^2
=\lambda-\hbar^2m(m+1)\ge0.
$$

If the ladder continued upward forever while $\lambda$ stayed fixed, the
right-hand side would eventually become negative. Therefore the ladder must
end at some $m=j$, where $J_+|j,j\rangle=0$. At that endpoint,

$$
0=\hbar^2j(j+1)-\hbar^2j(j+1),
$$

which fixes the Casimir eigenvalue to $\hbar^2j(j+1)$. Repeating the argument
at the bottom gives $m=-j$. Because the spacing is one, $2j$ must be a
nonnegative integer:

$$
j=0,\frac12,1,\frac32,\ldots,
\qquad m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j.
$$

The normalized action is

$$
J_\pm|j,m\rangle
=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle.
$$

This proof is a compact example of representation theory doing physics. A
commutator determines the step size, positivity forces endpoints, and the
distance between endpoints quantizes the representation.

## Why orbital angular momentum excludes half-integers

The abstract algebra permits both integer and half-integer $j$. A concrete
realization decides which ones occur.

For a scalar wavefunction on ordinary space, rotations act by moving the
argument:

$$
(U(R)\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(R^{-1}\mathbf r).
$$

Expanding an infinitesimal rotation gives the orbital generator

$$
\mathbf L=-i\hbar(\mathbf r\times\boldsymbol\nabla).
$$

In spherical coordinates,

$$
L_z=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},
$$

so an $L_z$ eigenfunction has azimuthal dependence $e^{im\phi}$. A scalar
wavefunction must return to the same value under $\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi$;
therefore $e^{2\pi im}=1$ and $m\in\mathbb Z$. The orbital multiplet then has
$\ell=0,1,2,\ldots$.

The remaining angular equation is

$$
L^2Y_{\ell m}=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m},
$$

whose regular, single-valued solutions on the sphere are the spherical
harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)$. They are not merely decorative orbital
shapes. They are matrix elements of rotation representations realized as
functions on $S^2$, and they form the natural Fourier basis for angular data.

## Spin lives in an internal representation

A spinor is not a scalar function that happens to carry half an orbital unit.
It takes values in an internal vector space. Rotations can therefore act both
on position and on components:

$$
\Psi(\mathbf r)\longmapsto
D(R)\Psi(R^{-1}\mathbf r).
$$

The orbital part is generated by $\mathbf L$; the internal matrix
representation is generated by $\mathbf S$. On the tensor product,

$$
\mathbf J=\mathbf L\otimes I+I\otimes\mathbf S.
$$

For spin one-half, the smallest nontrivial representation is two-dimensional.
The traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ matrices are spanned by the Pauli matrices,
and

$$
S_i=\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i
$$

satisfies the angular-momentum algebra. A $2\pi$ rotation sends a spinor to
minus itself, while a $4\pi$ rotation returns it. Probabilities are unchanged
by the first sign, but interference can detect it. This is the global
difference between $SO(3)$ and its double cover $SU(2)$ that the shared Lie
algebra alone does not reveal.

## Hydrogen: symmetry separates the problem

The relative electron-proton motion has Hamiltonian

$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r},
$$

with reduced mass $\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)$. Because the potential depends only
on $r$, $H$ commutes with $L^2$ and $L_z$. Write

$$
\psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).
$$

The angular equation has already been solved by representation theory. With
$u(r)=rR(r)$, the remaining radial equation becomes

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}
+\left[-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}
+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu.
$$

The second term in brackets is the centrifugal barrier. It is not another
force inserted by hand; it is the angular kinetic energy appearing as an
effective radial potential. Increasing $\ell$ suppresses probability near the
origin, which the lower interactive plot makes visible.

For a bound state, $u(r)$ must be regular at the origin and decay at infinity.
After extracting those endpoint behaviours, the remaining power series becomes
an associated Laguerre polynomial only if it terminates. That termination
condition gives

$$
E_n=-\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}\frac1{n^2}
\simeq-\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2},
$$

with $n=1,2,\ldots$, $\ell=0,1,\ldots,n-1$, and
$m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell$.

The number of orbital states at fixed $n$ is

$$
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1)=n^2.
$$

Rotation symmetry explains the $(2\ell+1)$ degeneracy within each $\ell$
multiplet. The larger fact that the nonrelativistic Coulomb energy depends on
$n$ but not $\ell$ is an additional, “accidental” degeneracy associated with
the special Coulomb problem. Spin, relativistic corrections, and the Lamb
shift further split levels that this model leaves degenerate.

## What this route buys us

One could solve the angular differential equation first and discover the same
integer labels. The algebra-first route reveals more:

1. Quantization begins before coordinates are chosen.
2. Integer versus half-integer values depend on the global representation, not
   merely on the local commutators.
3. Spherical harmonics and spinors are two realizations of the same generator
   algebra on different spaces.
4. Hydrogen energy quantization requires radial dynamics in addition to
   rotational symmetry.

That division of labour is the central lesson of the writeup. Symmetry tells us
how states organize. Boundary conditions and the Hamiltonian tell us which
organized states nature permits at a given energy.
