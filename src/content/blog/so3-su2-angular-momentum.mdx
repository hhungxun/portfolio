For $j=\tfrac12$, the formula

$$
J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle
$$

returns $\tfrac34\hbar^2$. The largest projection you can measure along any
axis is $\hbar/2$, so the naive guess for $J^2$ would be $\tfrac14\hbar^2$.
The formula is three times larger. Most first courses hand the result over to
be memorized, and if that is where you met it, the $j(j+1)$ probably looked
like something read off a spectroscopic table.

It follows instead from three structural facts: spatial rotations do not
commute, observable generators are Hermitian, and Hilbert-space norms cannot
be negative. The derivation is short and uses only the commutators.

Hydrogen adds a second layer on top of that one. Rotation symmetry determines
the angular functions and their allowed quantum numbers, while the Coulomb
potential and radial boundary conditions quantize the energy. The two layers
are worth keeping apart, because the $n^2$ degeneracy of hydrogen turns out to
be two separate facts and only one of them is a statement about symmetry.

## Rotations carry an algebra

An infinitesimal rotation by a small vector $\boldsymbol\theta$ acts on a
quantum state through

$$
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=I-\frac{i}{\hbar}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J
+O(|\boldsymbol\theta|^2).
$$

The operators $J_x,J_y,J_z$ are the generators of rotations. Turn a book 90°
about $x$ and then 90° about $y$, then start over and reverse the order: it
lands in a different orientation. The same failure survives all the way down to
infinitesimal rotations, where the mismatch is second order in the angle and is
itself a small rotation about $z$. Algebraically,

$$
[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k.
$$

This is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, equivalently
$\mathfrak{su}(2)$ at the infinitesimal level. First courses usually gloss it
as “the components cannot be measured together,” which is true and stops short
of the interesting part: each component generates a motion on state space, and
the commutator records the curvature of composing those motions.

The natural rotational scalar is

$$
J^2=J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2.
$$

Using $[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B$, one finds $[J^2,J_i]=0$. Thus $J^2$ is the
Casimir operator: within an irreducible representation it has one constant
value, while a chosen component such as $J_z$ distinguishes states inside the
multiplet. We may therefore seek simultaneous eigenstates

$$
J^2|\lambda,m\rangle=\lambda|\lambda,m\rangle,
\qquad J_z|\lambda,m\rangle=\hbar m|\lambda,m\rangle.
$$

## Ladder operators are eigen-operators

We want an operator $A$ that changes the $J_z$ eigenvalue by a fixed amount:

$$
[J_z,A]=\lambda A.
$$

Trying $A=\alpha J_x+\beta J_y$ and inserting the rotation commutators gives
$\lambda=\pm\hbar$ and $\beta=\pm i\alpha$. Up to normalization, the only
solutions are

$$
J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y,
\qquad [J_z,J_\pm]=\pm\hbar J_\pm.
$$

The only input was $[J_z,A]=\lambda A$. Solving it hands back $J_\pm$, the
eigenvectors of the adjoint action $A\mapsto[J_z,A]$. Applied to a state, they
shift $m$ by one and leave $J^2$ alone.

Two identities make the spectrum visible:

$$
J_-J_+=J^2-J_z^2-\hbar J_z,
\qquad
J_+J_-=J^2-J_z^2+\hbar J_z.
$$

Take the norm of a raised state:

$$
\|J_+|\lambda,m\rangle\|^2
=\lambda-\hbar^2m(m+1)\ge0.
$$

Worth pausing on that inequality before reading on. As we climb, $\lambda$ is
fixed and $m$ grows by one at every step. What stops it?

If the ladder continued upward forever while $\lambda$ stayed fixed, the
right-hand side would eventually become negative. Therefore the ladder must
end at some $m=j$, where $J_+|j,j\rangle=0$. At that endpoint,

$$
0=\hbar^2j(j+1)-\hbar^2j(j+1),
$$

which fixes the Casimir eigenvalue to $\hbar^2j(j+1)$. Repeating the argument
at the bottom gives $m=-j$. Because the spacing is one, $2j$ must be a
nonnegative integer:

$$
j=0,\frac12,1,\frac32,\ldots,
\qquad m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j.
$$

The normalized action is

$$
J_\pm|j,m\rangle
=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle.
$$

Take $j=\tfrac32$ as a worked case. The ladder has four rungs, and at the top
rung $m=\tfrac32$ both $j(j+1)$ and $m(m+1)$ equal $\tfrac{15}{4}$, so the
coefficient under the square root is exactly zero.

## Why orbital angular momentum excludes half-integers

The algebra permits both integer and half-integer $j$, and so far we have seen
nothing that prefers either. Which ones occur is decided by the concrete
realization.

For a scalar wavefunction on ordinary space, rotations act by moving the
argument:

$$
(U(R)\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(R^{-1}\mathbf r).
$$

Expanding an infinitesimal rotation gives the orbital generator

$$
\mathbf L=-i\hbar(\mathbf r\times\boldsymbol\nabla).
$$

In spherical coordinates,

$$
L_z=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},
$$

so an $L_z$ eigenfunction has azimuthal dependence $e^{im\phi}$. A scalar
wavefunction must return to the same value under $\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi$;
therefore $e^{2\pi im}=1$ and $m\in\mathbb Z$. Half-integers fail at the first
turn: $m=\tfrac12$ gives $e^{i\pi}=-1$. The orbital multiplet is left with
$\ell=0,1,2,\ldots$.

The remaining angular equation is

$$
L^2Y_{\ell m}=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m},
$$

whose regular, single-valued solutions on the sphere are the spherical
harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)$. These are the functions behind the $s$,
$p$ and $d$ orbital pictures in a chemistry textbook, which makes them easy to
file away as shapes. They are matrix elements of rotation representations
realized as functions on $S^2$, and they form the natural Fourier basis for
angular data.

## Spin lives in an internal representation

A spinor takes values in an internal vector space, which is what lets it carry
the half-integer $j$ that scalar functions on space just ruled out. Rotations
can therefore act both on position and on components:

$$
\Psi(\mathbf r)\longmapsto
D(R)\Psi(R^{-1}\mathbf r).
$$

The orbital part is generated by $\mathbf L$; the internal matrix
representation is generated by $\mathbf S$. On the tensor product,

$$
\mathbf J=\mathbf L\otimes I+I\otimes\mathbf S.
$$

For spin one-half, the smallest nontrivial representation is two-dimensional.
The traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ matrices are spanned by the Pauli matrices,
and

$$
S_i=\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i
$$

satisfies the angular-momentum algebra. A $2\pi$ rotation sends a spinor to
minus itself; you need $4\pi$ to get back. If that sounds like bookkeeping,
look at where the sign survives: probabilities are blind to it, interference is
not. $SO(3)$ and $SU(2)$ share a Lie algebra and differ globally, and this is
the place that difference becomes measurable.

## Hydrogen: symmetry separates the problem

The relative electron-proton motion has Hamiltonian

$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r},
$$

with reduced mass $\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)$. Because the potential depends only
on $r$, $H$ commutes with $L^2$ and $L_z$. Write

$$
\psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).
$$

The angular equation has already been solved by representation theory. With
$u(r)=rR(r)$, the remaining radial equation becomes

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}
+\left[-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}
+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu.
$$

The second term in brackets is the centrifugal barrier, and at this point it
reasonably looks like a force inserted to make the radial problem come out. It
is the angular kinetic energy showing up as an effective radial potential, and
the $\ell(\ell+1)$ sitting in it is the same $\ell(\ell+1)$ the ladder argument
produced. Increasing $\ell$ suppresses probability near the origin, which the
lower interactive plot makes visible.

For a bound state, $u(r)$ must be regular at the origin and decay at infinity.
After extracting those endpoint behaviours, the remaining power series becomes
an associated Laguerre polynomial only if it terminates. That termination
condition gives

$$
E_n=-\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}\frac1{n^2}
\simeq-\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2},
$$

with $n=1,2,\ldots$, $\ell=0,1,\ldots,n-1$, and
$m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell$.

The number of orbital states at fixed $n$ is

$$
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1)=n^2.
$$

Rotation symmetry explains the $(2\ell+1)$ degeneracy within each $\ell$
multiplet. The larger fact that the nonrelativistic Coulomb energy depends on
$n$ but not $\ell$ is an additional, “accidental” degeneracy associated with
the special Coulomb problem; at $n=2$ it is what puts $2s$ and $2p$ at the same
energy. Spin, relativistic corrections, and the Lamb shift further split levels
that this model leaves degenerate.

## What this route buys us

One could solve the angular differential equation first and arrive at the same
integer labels. Going through the algebra first buys three things that route
leaves buried.

Quantization begins before coordinates are chosen — the ladder argument never
mentions a sphere. The split between integer and half-integer $j$ turns out to
be global rather than local, decided by which group is being represented, since
the commutators are identical either way. And spherical harmonics and spinors
are one generator algebra realized on two different spaces, which is why the
$\ell(\ell+1)$ of the angular equation and the $j(j+1)$ of the ladder are the
same expression.

Hydrogen is where the method reaches its limit, and that is worth saying as
plainly as the successes. Its energies need the radial dynamics; symmetry alone
gets you the angular labels and stops there. Symmetry organizes the states, and
the Hamiltonian and the boundary conditions decide which of them nature permits
at a given energy.
